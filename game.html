The “about 2%” calculation

The number is useful only when the denominator is explicit.

IPCC AR6 benchmark · through 2019
445 ÷ 18,635 = 2.39%

IPCC AR6 Working Group I reports cumulative fossil-fuel and industry CO₂ emissions of 445 ± 20 PgC over 1750–2019 and fossil-fuel resources of 11,490 PgC coal + 6,780 PgC oil + 365 PgC natural gas = 18,635 PgC.

Updated illustrative context · through 2025
(495 + 10.4) ÷ 18,635 ≈ 2.71%

The Global Carbon Budget 2025 gives about 495 ± 25 GtC cumulative fossil CO₂ emissions for 1850–2024 and about 10.4 GtC projected fossil CO₂ emissions in 2025. Comparing that with the older IPCC resource denominator yields roughly 2.7%.

Interpretation boundary: this does not mean humanity has used only 2% of “all carbon,” and it does not imply that the remaining 97–98% is economically recoverable or climate-safe to burn. The comparison illustrates the scale of the upstream geological stock relative to historical transfer.

Model boundary

A teaching game, not a climate forecast.

The Policy Lab does not model carbon-cycle feedbacks, changing sink efficiency, ocean chemistry, temperature response, non-CO₂ forcing, or socioeconomic dynamics. Its purpose is to show the logic of stocks, inflows, outflows, and scale.

Core lesson

Reducing emissions does not automatically make atmospheric CO₂ decline. The bathtub falls only when total removals exceed total inflow.

System Dynamics takeaway

The binding constraint is not whether the upstream tank can be emptied. It is how fast carbon is transferred into the active atmosphere–land–ocean system.

A geologically modest transfer can produce a major atmospheric change because the atmosphere is a comparatively small stock. That is why flow control matters long before the upstream geological resource frame is close to exhaustion.
