Why is the fossil-carbon faucet still open?

This laboratory makes the mechanisms between policy and emissions visible. Instead of letting a carbon-price slider directly change atmospheric CO₂, it passes through expected profitability, capital investment and retirement, production capacity, extraction, fuel inventory, combustion, substitution, and institutional persistence.

Model statusHypothesis sandbox · not calibrated
Interpretation boundary: the upstream behavioral coefficients remain largely uncalibrated. The atmospheric response now uses a reduced four-timescale carbon-cycle emulator with Joos/IPCC response coefficients and one historically fitted lifetime scale (α = 0.555). It reproduces the 1959–2024 atmospheric stock with ≈1.19 ppm RMSE, but it is still not a full Earth-system model or a forecast. Scenario differences remain conditional on the upstream assumptions.
Why this structure?

Separate extraction from emission.

The advanced engine explicitly passes fossil carbon through extraction, process emissions, a fuel-inventory stock, combustion, non-combustion product use, and delayed product decay. This prevents the common shortcut of treating extraction as instant atmospheric emission.

The atmospheric response uses four carbon-response pools based on the Joos/IPCC impulse-response coefficients. Their decay timescales are multiplied by a single historically calibrated lifetime scale, following the reduced-form logic used by FaIR. The model diagnoses combined net natural uptake from the carbon balance rather than imposing a fixed fraction of current emissions.

Evidence

Lock-in is supported; these coefficients are not.

Unruh (2000) and Seto et al. (2016) describe path dependence and interacting technological, infrastructural, institutional, and behavioral forms of carbon lock-in. Tong et al. (2019) quantifies committed emissions from existing infrastructure.

Those studies motivate the feedback hypotheses. They do not provide one global parameterization for the equations shown here.
