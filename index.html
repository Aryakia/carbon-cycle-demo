Carbon Beyond the Bathtub

The familiar bathtub analogy is useful, but incomplete. Atmospheric CO₂ is the water level in one important stock. Upstream sits a much larger geological fossil-carbon stock, while land and ocean act as large connected reservoirs and sinks. This game lets you move through time, watch the atmospheric level rise, and see why controlling the inflow matters long before the upstream stock is close to empty.

The “about 2%” calculation

Useful—but only with the denominator stated.

IPCC AR6 benchmark · through 2019
445 ÷ 18,635 = 2.39%

IPCC AR6 reports cumulative fossil-fuel and industry emissions of 445 ± 20 PgC over 1750–2019, and fossil-fuel resources of 11,490 PgC coal + 6,780 PgC oil + 365 PgC gas = 18,635 PgC. In that specific comparison, only about 2.4% of the resource-frame carbon had been transferred as cumulative fossil/industry emissions by 2019.

Updated context · 2024/2025
(495 + 10.4) ÷ 18,635 ≈ 2.71%

Global Carbon Budget 2025 estimates 495 ± 25 GtC cumulative fossil CO₂ emissions for 1850–2024 and projected about 10.4 GtC of fossil emissions in 2025. Compared against the older IPCC resource denominator, the illustrative fraction is now about 2.7%.

Do not read this as “we have only used 2% of Earth’s carbon” or “98% is available to burn.” The denominator here is IPCC’s fossil-fuel resource estimate. It says something about the huge upstream geological stock relative to historical transfer—not about economic recoverability, policy feasibility, or the remaining carbon budget compatible with a temperature target.

The Global Carbon Budget 2025 estimates that during 2015–2024, about 50% of total anthropogenic CO₂ emissions accumulated in the atmosphere, while the ocean absorbed about 29% and land about 21%. The game uses a 50% sink-share default for the future sandbox as a communication starting point, not as a forecast.

The scenario does not simulate carbon-cycle feedbacks, ocean chemistry, temperature, non-CO₂ forcing, or changing sink efficiency.

Core systems lesson

The constraint is not whether the upstream tank can be emptied. The constraint is how much carbon can be moved into the fast atmosphere–land–ocean system without unacceptable consequences.

The leverage point is the transfer rate: fossil extraction and combustion move carbon from a slow geological stock into a much faster surface carbon cycle. The atmosphere is a relatively small stock, so a geologically modest transfer can produce a large concentration change.
